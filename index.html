Travel

Getting to Singapore, settling in and getting around.

Plan your trip

When to arrive

Fly into Singapore Changi Airport, the only airport you need. Singapore is 16 hours ahead of Seattle and 13 ahead of Bogotá, so arrive at least three days early if you can: by Wednesday 24 February.

Where to stay

Staying at Hilton Singapore Orchard puts you an elevator ride from the day. Anywhere along Orchard Road or near a North South Line station is an easy trip.

Hotel on Google Maps

Weather in late February

Warm and humid, around 25 to 31°C (77 to 88°F), with the odd short shower. Indoors is cool, so a light layer is useful.

Before you fly

To enter Singapore

Passport
Valid for at least six months.
Visa
Not needed for a short visit on a US or Colombian passport. Other nationalities: check the official ICA entry requirements.
Arrival card
Submit the free SG Arrival Card within three days before you land, on the official ICA site or the MyICA app. Sites that charge a fee are not ICA.
From Colombia
Been in Colombia in the six days before you land, including a layover of more than 12 hours? Bring a yellow fever vaccination certificate.

Sort out mobile data

You will need data for maps and for booking rides once you land. Set up a tourist eSIM at home, plan to buy a SIM at the airport, or use your home carrier’s roaming. SIM and eSIM options

Set up a ride app

Download one or two ride-hailing apps and add a card while your phone can still receive verification texts, and tell your bank you will be in Singapore. Which apps, and how to set them up

What to pack

Light clothes, something that covers shoulders and knees for temples and mosques, a compact umbrella, one light layer, an insulated bottle (fill it with ice in the morning), and a UK-style three-pin plug adapter. The FAQ covers whether you also need a voltage converter.

Local SIM and eSIM options

A Singapore number and data plan is cheap and makes the ride apps and maps easy. Your phone must be unlocked.

  • eSIM before you fly: StarHub’s tourist eSIM can be bought online at home, on the phone that will use it, with a passport check in the app. Activate it after you land, within 18 days of buying. M1 and Singtel also sell tourist eSIMs online; some Singtel plans start counting days from purchase, so don’t buy too early.
  • Physical SIM at Changi: counters in the arrival hall of every terminal (Changi Recommends, StarHub and others), most open 24 hours. Bring your passport; it is needed to register the SIM. Changi’s SIM card guide
  • Plans run about S$10 to S$30 for 7 to 30 days with plenty of data. Check the operators’ pages for current prices.
  • Or use your home carrier’s roaming. Free Wi-Fi at Changi helps you set everything up when you land.

When you land

1. Clear immigration

With your SG Arrival Card submitted, immigration is usually quick.

2. Get connected

Activate your eSIM, or pick up a tourist SIM at a counter in the arrival hall. Bring your passport to the counter.

3. Get to the hotel

Taxi: taxi stands at every terminal, roughly 25 to 30 minutes and about S$25 to S$45 to Orchard Road (there is an airport surcharge). Or book a private hire car in an app; Changi has marked pick-up points.
MRT: about an hour. Change at Tanah Merah, then at City Hall for the North South Line to Somerset.

Distilled water for CPAP machines

If you use a CPAP, you can pick up distilled water as soon as you land, before leaving the airport.

  1. 1
    After customs, find a Cheers convenience store in the public arrival area. They are open 24 hours: Terminal 3 Arrival Hall, #01-24 and Terminal 4 Arrival Hall, #01-09. Arriving at Terminal 1 or 2? Go up one floor to the Cheers in the Level 2 departure hall. All Changi convenience stores
  2. 2
    Look for bottles labelled “distilled”, not “drinking” or “mineral” water.
  3. 3
    Back-up: FairPrice Finest at Jewel (Basement 2, 9 am to 11 pm), reached from Terminal 1 or by link bridge from Terminals 2 and 3, is the best back-up: FairPrice sells distilled water (look for the Life brand). Bringing one night’s worth from home is a good safety net.

Getting around Singapore

MRT and buses

Fast, cheap and air-conditioned. Tap a contactless Visa, Mastercard or Amex, or your phone, at the gate and on the bus. Each person needs their own card or phone. Tap in and out with the same one. Foreign cards pay a small fee of S$0.60 per day of travel.

To avoid foreign card fees, get a local EZ-Link card instead. It costs S$10, including S$5 of credit, at 7-Eleven or the ticket office in Changi Airport MRT station. Top it up with cash at station machines or 7-Eleven.

Taxis

Taxis are metered and can be flagged down on the street, or found at taxi stands. They cannot stop at bus stops or in bus lanes, and in the city centre use a taxi stand or a building’s driveway. Expect surcharges at peak times, late at night and from the airport. Most take cards.

Private hire cars

This is what Singapore calls Uber-style rides (Uber itself left in 2018). They must be booked in an app, cannot be flagged down, and show the fare before you ride. Which apps to use

Do

Singapore is small, safe and easy to get around. If you are flying this far, stay a while. Here is what we would do with the time.

If you only have a few days
  • Eat at a hawker centre, more than once. Where to go and what to order
  • Take the MRT. Preston loves the trains here, and they will get you to almost everything on this page. How to ride
  • See the Night Safari at Mandai. Book a time slot ahead. It opens at 6 pm and is about an hour from Orchard.
  • Go to Gardens by the Bay: we recommend both the Cloud Forest and the Flower Dome, then stay for the free Supertree light show at 7:45 or 8:45 pm. Each dome closes one day a month: check the dates.
  • Spend a morning each in Chinatown, Little India and Kampong Gelam. What to see in each
  • Start one morning with a walk. The Botanic Gardens at dawn is the easy one; for a real hike, see Walks and hikes.
  • Walk around Toa Payoh, where Charlotte grew up and a classic HDB (public housing) town, to see how most Singaporeans live.
Close to the hotel
  • Emerald Hill. A lane of restored Peranakan terrace houses five minutes’ walk away, best in the evening.
  • Fort Canning Park. A small historic hill and the best first-morning walk. About 20 minutes on foot, or one MRT stop from Somerset to Dhoby Ghaut.
  • Singapore Botanic Gardens. Two MRT stops from Orchard station (Napier). Free, open from 5 am, and perfect for a jet-lagged early start. Look out for the wild chickens, actually red junglefowl, wandering the lawns.
Neighbourhoods

Chinatown, Little India and Kampong Gelam are the historic quarters of Singapore’s three main communities. Each is a good half day, and all are 15 to 20 minutes from Orchard by MRT.

  • Chinatown (morning; Chinatown or Maxwell MRT). The Buddha Tooth Relic Temple, the Hindu Sri Mariamman Temple and the Hokkien Thian Hock Keng temple sit within a few streets of each other. Cover shoulders and knees for all three, and leave your shoes at the door at Sri Mariamman. Lunch at Maxwell Food Centre.
  • Little India (morning or after dark; Little India MRT). Tekka Centre for food and the wet market, the Sri Veeramakaliamman Temple (shoulders and knees covered, shoes off at the door), flower garland stalls, and Mustafa Centre, a department store that sells everything and never seems to close.
  • Kampong Gelam (late afternoon into evening; Bugis MRT). The golden-domed Sultan Mosque (shoulders and knees covered, shoes off at the door), the textile shops of Arab Street, and the murals and boutiques of Haji Lane. During Ramadan a night bazaar sets up here.
  • Marina Bay (5 pm onwards; Bayfront MRT). Not historic, but the skyline everyone comes for. Walk the waterfront loop at sunset, then cross to Gardens by the Bay for the Supertree light show.
Walks and hikes

The heat doubles the effort. Start by 8 am and finish by 11, or go after 4:30 pm. Bring an insulated bottle filled with ice, a hat and a small umbrella.

  • Sungei Buloh Wetland Reserve. Charlotte’s pick. Flat mangrove boardwalks with monitor lizards, mudskippers and the occasional crocodile. Open 7 am to 7 pm. From Kranji MRT, take bus 925 (925M on Sundays and public holidays). Allow about an hour each way from Orchard.
  • MacRitchie TreeTop Walk. Preston’s pick. A rainforest trail out to a 250-metre suspension bridge in the canopy; from the Venus Drive entrance the full loop is about 7 km, 3 to 4 hours. Go in the morning: the bridge is one-way, closed on Mondays, and opens at 9 am Tuesday to Friday and 8:30 am at weekends, with last entry at 4:45 pm. The monkeys here are bold: keep food and plastic bags out of sight.
  • Southern Ridges. Hilltop parks joined by bridges, including Henderson Waves, with harbour views. About 10 km end to end; start from HarbourFront MRT and walk as much of it as you like.
Public housing estates worth seeing

About 77% of households live in HDB flats: public housing built by the Housing & Development Board. Seeing an HDB town, with its void decks, playgrounds, markets and hawker centre, is seeing everyday Singapore.

  • Toa Payoh (Toa Payoh MRT, about 10 minutes from Orchard, no change). Where Charlotte grew up. The first town designed and built entirely by HDB, and still a busy, typical one. Follow the heritage trail (about 1.5 hours on foot) past the 1979 Dragon Playground at Block 28 Lorong 6 and Block 53, the “VIP block” Queen Elizabeth visited in 1972, then eat at Lorong 8 Market & Food Centre.
  • Tiong Bahru estate. Built before the HDB existed, by its predecessor. Curved Art Deco flats from the 1930s to 50s, spiral staircases, and a neighbourhood market and cafés. See it in 60 pictures or follow the Tiong Bahru Heritage Trail. Pair it with breakfast at Tiong Bahru Market.
  • Queenstown. Singapore’s first satellite estate, begun in 1952. HDB’s very first blocks, from 1960, still stand on Stirling Road; the My Queenstown Heritage Trail maps out what to see.
For the board gamers

We play a lot when we are in Singapore. Where we go:

  • Geekster (Geylang, near Aljunied MRT). Our favourite place to play. It is community-focused, welcoming and friendly, and plays the strategy games we like. On Thursday strategy nights you can roll two dice to set your entry fee. It is where we found Wandering Towers. Closed Mondays and Wednesdays, and evenings only on other weekdays.
  • King and the Pawn (Purvis Street, near City Hall and Bugis MRT). Modern, keeps up with the board game industry, and caters to strategy games, with decent food to eat while you play. The space is small and fills up quickly, so book. Evenings only Tuesday to Friday, from 11 am at weekends. They made Fú: Festive Fortunes and sell it.
  • The Mind Cafe (30 Prinsep Street). We have played here a lot: a big open space, easy to find seats, and a good library to borrow from. The crowd skews casual, so it can get loud with groups playing party games.
  • GuildHall (15 Phillip Street, near Raffles Place MRT). Good for playing; they do not sell games.

To buy games: The Mind Cafe (the same Prinsep Street store), Team Board Game (near MacPherson MRT; the shop closes at 8 pm, 6 pm on Sundays) and Geekster.

Museums, and what to do when it rains

Start with the National Museum for the story of Singapore, then pick by interest. Museums are also the best answer to the midday heat or a rainstorm.

  • National Museum of Singapore. The best single primer. Its main Singapore History Gallery reopened in October 2026 after a three-year rebuild.
  • Peranakan Museum. The culture of the Straits Chinese: beadwork, porcelain, wedding traditions. Small and beautiful.
  • Asian Civilisations Museum. Trade, faith and craft across Asia, in a riverside colonial building.
  • National Gallery Singapore. Go for the building: the former Supreme Court and City Hall joined under a glass roof. Its main Southeast Asian gallery is closed until late 2027.
For the F1 fans

The Singapore Grand Prix is a night race in October, so there is no racing while you are here. But the Marina Bay Street Circuit is mostly ordinary city road, and you can walk most of the lap any day. It is about 5 km and best at dusk. Start at Promenade MRT and the Pit Building, and finish at the Singapore Flyer, which looks straight down on the pit straight.

Happening while you are here
  • Ramadan night bazaars at Geylang Serai and Kampong Gelam. Ramadan is expected to run from 8 February to 9 March, so they should be on, though the 2027 bazaar dates are not announced yet. Go after 4 pm, or after sunset when they are liveliest, and go hungry.
  • Gardens by the Bay maintenance closures. Cloud Forest on Tuesday 23 February, Floral Fantasy on Wednesday 24 February, Flower Dome on Tuesday 2 March, OCBC Skyway on Wednesday 3 March.
What we would skip
  • Universal Studios Singapore. Small, and many of its rides exist at home. A fair choice with kids; otherwise skip it.

Eat

Singapore is extremely good at feeding people, and hawker centres are the move. Here is where we would eat, and what to order.

Hawker centres: where to go

Hawker centres are open-air food halls where each stall has spent decades on one or two dishes, and most plates cost S$4 to S$8. No tax, no service charge, no tipping.

  • Lau Pa Sat. Our favourite, for the balance of convenience and variety, though a little pricier than most. In the evening the road outside becomes Satay Street, lined with satay grills from 7 pm on weekdays and 3 pm at weekends. It gets very crowded, so go early. Raffles Place MRT, direct from Somerset.
  • Maxwell Food Centre. The easiest first visit. Orchard to Maxwell MRT on the Thomson-East Coast Line, no change. Next to Chinatown.
  • Tiong Bahru Market. Breakfast in a pretty old neighbourhood; go between 8 and 11 am. Pairs with a walk round the Tiong Bahru estate. Havelock MRT, direct from Orchard, or Tiong Bahru MRT.
  • Old Airport Road Food Centre. The one locals argue is the best. About 30 minutes by MRT to Dakota, with one change; worth it for the food.
What to order

Most dishes are small and cheap, so order widely and share.

  • Hainanese chicken rice. Poached chicken on rice cooked in chicken stock, with chilli and ginger on the side.
  • Satay. Grilled skewers with a sweet peanut sauce.
  • Hokkien mee. Noodles braised in prawn stock. Savoury and seafood-rich, not spicy.
  • Roti prata. Flaky griddled flatbread, dipped in curry.
  • Kaya toast and kopi. The classic breakfast: toast with coconut jam and butter, soft-boiled eggs, and local coffee.
  • Nasi lemak. Coconut rice with fried chicken, egg and sweet-spicy sambal.
  • Char kway teow. Smoky stir-fried flat rice noodles in sweet dark soy.
  • Carrot cake. No carrot, and not a cake: fried cubes of radish cake with egg. Order “white” (savoury) or “black” (sweet soy).
  • Chendol. Shaved ice with coconut milk and palm sugar, for when the heat wins.
How a hawker centre works

Find a table first (a tissue packet on a table means it is taken), order and pay at the stall, and return your tray when you finish. Bring small cash, and a packet of tissues or wet wipes: napkins are not provided, and you may want to wipe down your seat or table.

Our story

How we met, and how we got here.

Preston and I met on the apps in May 2024. For our first date, he skipped the restaurant and cooked me a grand meal, with swordfish he had bought fresh that day. I had never eaten swordfish before, and it was delicious. Looking back, that dinner told me most of what I needed to know about him.

He has been that thoughtful ever since. He keeps an emergency stash of mushrooms in the kitchen because he knows how much I love them, and he often surprises me with my favourite bubble tea, chicken rice or sushi. He notices the small things too, like whether my knitting is making progress or being undone. Once, at a board game convention, maple syrup leaked all through my backpack just as my game was starting. He dropped everything to clean it up, made sure my knitting was safe first, and turned a bad morning into a good day.

About six months in, we started making a little board game of our own called Woodland Home, starring our two cats. We worked on it for a year and a half, and I thought I knew every card. Then, on a sunny day in May 2026, he insisted we take it to the woods for a picnic and a game. I convinced him that a park was woodsy enough.

Partway through the game, the cat card turned up with an instruction I had never seen: open envelope A once both cats have been revealed. Inside was a lovely letter about our life together, and then he asked me to marry him. I said yes, of course. He told me afterwards that the day we started the game was the day he knew.

The picnic was just as carefully planned. There were vegetables because I had been saying I needed more of them, proper utensils because I don’t like eating finger food with my hands, and tiny champagne bottles he chose because they were shiny and he knows I love shiny things.

We feel very lucky to have found each other, and even luckier to celebrate with the people we love, in Singapore and in Seattle. We can’t wait to see you.

FAQ

The day in Singapore

What time should I arrive?

Please arrive by 10:00 am. Everyone is invited to the tea ceremony at 10:00 in the Mahogany Room on Level 5, followed by the vow exchange at about 11:00. Lunch begins at 12:30 pm and we finish at about 3:00 pm.

Where exactly is it?

Hilton Singapore Orchard, 333 Orchard Road, Singapore 238867. The tea ceremony and vows are in the Mahogany Room and lunch is at Estate, both inside the hotel. Open in Google Maps

What should I wear?

Dress as you would for a special lunch at a nice hotel: a dress, or a shirt with trousers, and a jacket if you like one. Choose light, breathable fabrics for the trip over, and bring a wrap or light layer because the hotel is kept cool. Comfortable shoes are fine. There is no need for anything formal or floor-length.

What should I expect on the day?

A relaxed morning and a long lunch, all inside the hotel: the tea ceremony, a short exchange of vows, then a buffet lunch at Estate. Seating is open, so sit wherever you like in our reserved section. We have not booked out the whole restaurant: Estate stays open to hotel guests and the public, so there will be other diners around us. There is no long programme to sit through, and we will come round to every table.

What is a tea ceremony?

A Chinese wedding tradition. The couple serves tea to parents and elders in order of seniority as a mark of respect, and the elders give their blessings in return. All guests are invited.

What is an ang bao, and should I bring one?

No, please don’t feel you need to. An ang bao (say “ahng pow”; also called a red packet) is a red envelope with a gift of money inside, given at Chinese weddings, at Lunar New Year and on other happy occasions. Red is the colour of good luck. It is the customary wedding gift in Singapore, so you will see guests drop envelopes into a box at the welcome table, and elders handing them to us during the tea ceremony.

None of this is expected of you, least of all if you are flying in from the US. You have already come a very long way, and having you there is the gift.

Can I bring my children?

Yes. Please include them in your RSVP so the restaurant has the right numbers.

Can I bring a plus-one?

Yes, plus-ones are welcome. Just add their name to your RSVP.

What about dietary needs?

Lunch is a buffet at Estate with plenty of choice, so there is usually something for most diets. We cannot arrange special meals or control allergens in the kitchen, so if you have a serious allergy, please ask the restaurant staff about each dish on the day.

Travel

What will the weather be like?

Singapore in late February is warm and humid, around 25 to 31°C (77 to 88°F), with the occasional short, heavy shower. A small umbrella is worth packing. The whole day is indoors and air-conditioned, so you will only feel the heat getting there.

Do I need a visa for Singapore?

Not for a short visit on a US or Colombian passport. Everyone must submit the free SG Arrival Card online within three days before arriving, and your passport should be valid for at least six months. Rules differ by nationality, so if you hold another passport please check the official ICA entry requirements before you book.

Do I need a yellow fever vaccination?

Only if you have been in Colombia, or another country with yellow fever risk, in the six days before you land; a layover of more than 12 hours counts. You will need a vaccination certificate, and the vaccine must be given at least 10 days before you arrive, so see your doctor early. ICA’s yellow fever rules

Is there anything I should leave at home?
  • Vapes and e-cigarettes. They are illegal even to possess, and fines are heavy.
  • Cannabis and CBD products, including anything legal in your state.
  • Some prescription medicines, unless approved in advance. Up to three months’ supply of your own prescription medicine is fine, in its labelled pharmacy container, with your prescription or a doctor’s letter. But anything containing a controlled substance needs approval from the Health Sciences Authority, applied for at least two weeks before you arrive. That includes ADHD medicines (such as Adderall, Vyvanse or Ritalin), strong painkillers with codeine or oxycodone, and anxiety or sleep medicines (such as Xanax, Valium, Klonopin or Ambien). HSA’s full rules

How do I get around?

Do I need a plug adapter or a voltage converter?

Everyone needs a plug adapter: Singapore uses UK-style three-pin plugs and 230V power. Phone, laptop and camera chargers almost always say “100–240V” on the label, so the adapter is all they need. Anything that says only 110V or 120V, often hair dryers and straighteners, would also need a voltage converter; it is usually easier to leave those at home.
